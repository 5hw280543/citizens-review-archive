## Corrected Dataset

### Relevant Context
- The 2016/2018 Ladysmith Wastewater Treatment Plant (WWTP) operates at a secondary treatment level and does not utilize tertiary (third-stage) treatment. It is often mistaken for having "Stage 3" treatment due to the completion of "Phase 3" in the plant’s development.
  > "The culmination of the upgrade program, Phase 3, was completed in 2017 and included a new secondary treatment facility" *(Source: WSP Engineering Project Logs)*

- The 2016/2018 Ladysmith Wastewater Treatment Plant (WWTP) increased the previous plant’s capacity and is able to discharge up to 7x the average flow of effluent into the harbour.
  > "As shown, the ratio of MDF:ADWF was in the range 4.5:1 to 7:1, indicating that I&I to the WWTP collection system is excessive according to the MSR criterion" *(Source: Town of Ladysmith Liquid Waste Management Plan Stages 1 and 2, Section 1.1.1)*

- The Ladysmith WWTP’s 2018 Award of Excellence is an "Award of Merit for Category 2 Municipal and Civil Infrastructure" for the building’s design, and was self-submitted by the engineering firm WSP/Opus. It is not an indication of function nor processing effectiveness.
  > "The award submission was brought forward on the Town's behalf by Opus International Consultants (Canada) Ltd., the engineering firm responsible for the design, construction and commissioning of the upgraded facility... Category 2 Municipal and Civil Infrastructure... WSP | Opus designed all phases of the upgrade. Phase 3 was completed in 2017 and included the construction of a new multi-level building, enclosing the secondary treatment processes with optimal odour control." *(Source: Association of Consulting Engineering Companies Project Archives)*

### Supporting Evidence, Harbour-001-2: Transfer Beach Advisory, Town of Ladysmith

[Public commentary transcript regarding liveaboards, May 30, 2025](https://www.facebook.com)
- **Relevant Excerpt:**  
  > "Blaming the 20 liveaboard boats in Ladysmith Harbour for the bacterial contamination at Transfer Beach is not remotely feasible when examined logically and scientifically. Here's why:
  > 
  > ### 💧 **1. Discharge Volume Comparison:**
  > * **Liveaboard Boats (20 total):** Each boat might produce ~20–40 litres of blackwater (sewage) per day. Even if every boat illegally dumped, that’s 400–800 litres/day total.
  > * **Town of Ladysmith Effluent:** The wastewater treatment plant discharges millions of litres per day (e.g., ~2,000,000 L/day is common for small towns). Even with treatment, treated effluent can still contain bacteria, especially during storm overflow or high tide backflows.
  > * **Scale matters**: Town discharge is literally thousands of times greater in volume than what boats could produce.
  > 
  > ### 🌊 **2. Proximity and Hydrodynamics:**
  > * Transfer Beach is directly adjacent to the municipal outfall area. Even treated effluent can cause elevated fecal coliform or E. coli counts if it doesn’t disperse well — and during spring low tides, shallow water, warm temperatures, and stagnant conditions increase bacterial survival.
  > * Most liveaboard boats are moored farther out, and the water between them and the beach is well-circulated by tides.
  > 
  > ### 🧪 **3. Testing & Source Tracing:**
  > * DNA-based microbial source tracking can distinguish between human, avian, or canine fecal sources. If testing was done and showed human markers, it still wouldn’t distinguish between treated town effluent and boat sewage unless further testing was done — and most such outbreaks match urban stormwater and treatment overflow patterns, not isolated boat anchorages.
  > 
  > ### 🧭 **4. Timing Correlation:**
  > * These beach closures almost always align with spring freshet or heavy rain (increasing town runoff), super low tides (exposing and warming sediment), and warmer water temps (boosting bacterial survival). There is no evidence that this small number of boats suddenly changed behavior in sync with those events.
  > 
  > ### 🧱 **5. Regulatory Oversight:**
  > * Many liveaboards use holding tanks or dock pump-outs. Random illegal discharges would need to be frequent, coordinated, and sustained, which is highly unlikely. Also, they would be traceable — yet no enforcement action has identified boats as a direct source.
  > 
  > ### ⚠️ **6. Misplaced Blame Masks Bigger Issues:**
  > * It’s easier to scapegoat 20 visible liveaboards than to confront inadequate stormwater infrastructure, overflow events from the treatment plant, aging sewer lines near the beach, and massive surface runoff from urban hardscapes.
  > 
  > ### ✅ Summary:
  > The idea that 20 liveaboard boats are the primary cause of bacterial closure at Transfer Beach collapses under scrutiny. Between the vastly greater volume of municipal discharge, the environmental conditions that favor town-origin contamination, and the lack of evidence implicating boats, it’s clear that blaming them is more about optics than science. If the town wants to protect the beach, it should improve wastewater treatment and storm overflow handling, enhance source-tracing efforts, and address shoreline infrastructure—not distract the public by blaming a small, easy target."
